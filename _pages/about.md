**Earth’s atmosphere** is the stuff of small talk (how about that storm?) and big questions: Why is Earth’s atmosphere the way it is? How is it changing? How should we think about these changes? 

The combination of **weather and climate** caught hold of me before I was even a teenager, as I pored over weather model output to predict when I could build the next snowfort while also learning that everyday actions by people like me will change the planet for millennia.

Now, I am an **atmospheric scientist** specializing in **stratosphere-troposphere dynamics and photochemistry**. I am also a **climate science communicator**. 

My work builds rigorous understanding of the atmosphere through the analysis of observations and a hierarchy of models ranging from pencil-and-paper theory to high-performance computer models.

I am currently a postdoc working with Peter Hitchcock at Cornell University. We are working to understand how surface warming affects the **tropical tropopause layer** and stratosphere. We are thinking carefully about how the effects of uniform versus patterned warming are mediated through changes in convection, the large-scale circulation, waves, ozone, and water vapor. A key tool we are developing, with Peter Blossey and Blaz Gasparini, is the first-ever cloud-resolving model with a (simplified) representation of ozone photochemistry, which will help us explore fundamental questions about the structure and sensitivities of an atmosphere in **radiative-convective-*photochemical* equilibrium**.

Previously, I was an NSF Postdoctoral Research Fellow in Atmospheric and Geospace Sciences with Ed Gerber at NYU. We developed a new explanation for the basic shape of the **ozone layer**, finding that textbook explanations in terms of photochemistry alone omitted the proper effects of transport. We discovered that part of Earth's upper stratosphere is *photochemically destabilizing*, i.e., ozone reductions aloft perturb ultraviolet fluxes to cause further ozone reductions below (the opposite of the well-known phenomenon of "self-healing", which occurs at lower altitudes). We also showed that model-predicted reductions in tropical lower stratospheric ozone due to global warming arise not only from strengthened upwelling, as was well known, but also from tropospheric expansion.

My Ph.D. is in Atmospheric and Oceanic Sciences from Princeton University where I was advised by Stephan Fueglistaler. We studied the dominant wind pattern in the tropical stratosphere, which is alternating easterly and westerly winds that descend with a period of about 28 months, called the **Quasi-Biennial Oscillation** (QBO). We revisited why the QBO doesn’t descend below 70 hPa (about 18.5 km), instead terminating in what has been called a **buffer zone**. We found that the classical explanation for the buffer zone had the answer improperly built in through a zero-wind lower boundary condition---a logical circularity. Breaking the circularity revealed that the lower stratospheric QBO vanishes because it is damped by lateral momentum exchange. In response to global warming, the buffer zone is expected to shift upwards with the expanding troposphere, weakening the QBO at a given altitude, yet this signal has not yet emerged.

Currently, uniting my interests in the QBO and ozone, I am the Community Lead for Working Group 3 of Quasi-biennial oscillation and Ozone Chemistry interactions in the Atmosphere (QUOCA), focused on Analytical Models and Linearized Ozone Parameterizations. Towards these efforts, Peter Hitchcock and I have been developing [ModPAC](https://github.com/aph42/ModPAC/) (Modular Photochemistry in an Atmospheric Column). I am also participating in an ISSI group on using observations to learn about **QBO-ozone interactions**. Reach out if you want to contribute to QUOCA WG3.

Broadly, I believe that climate science will be most useful to society if we (scientists) are open about our uncertainties and welcoming of diverse reactions to our results. Our scientific authority rests on trust that we must continually (re-)earn. I perform **climate outreach** with colleagues through the non-profit organization we co-founded in 2019 called [Climate Up Close](https://www.climateupclose.org/). We have gone on seven tours, each consisting of events where we share our understanding of what is certain and uncertain about climate change (benchmarked against the IPCC) in a non-prescriptive and non-judgmental way. We have reached thousands of Americans at places of worship, libraries, bars, and environmental centers. Our 2026 tour will be in North Carolina from October 9-12.

My outreach feeds back onto my research. I have been working with Benjamin Schaffer and Stephan Fueglistaler on the theory of **extreme event impact attribution**. This work resolves key interpretive questions, and poses new ones, about methods that have been proposed to be used in litigation, reparations, adaptation, and planning.
